# Muse Acts Under Your Seat, Never Alone

**Muse is a delegate, not a clone.** Read that way, a Muse is one agent per person that holds exactly that person's seat in Etyme — the same company, the same desk, the same permission words, and never more — and does a short list of things the person has signed it up to do, from the places the person already works: email, Teams, a text. It is not a copy of the person's voice or knowledge, it is not an Etyme bot, and it is not a new chat screen. Etyme already holds most of what such a delegate needs, because the product was built to prove to an auditor who did what and why: one permission list checked at one door on every API call, a log of every read and every refusal, a log of every unprompted act with a reason and an honest "can this be undone" flag, refusals written as sentences, a seat that one party grants to another with a trail (`ProgramSeat`), approval by a one-time email link, and a queue of "what needs a person right now" (`GET /api/decisions`). Five things are missing, and they are small: a row that records the person's grant to the twin (what, how much, until when, revoke), a name for "the twin did this for Priya" in the three logs, a place to keep a person's standing instructions, a dry-run on write routes, and a trail the person can read. Nobody in the market sells a twin that holds a person's authority inside a work record; the twin has been split into a *likeness* for talking (Delphi, Tavus) and an *account* with a human sponsor for calling systems (Microsoft, Okta, Google), and every shipped assistant that touches money asks a named human first ([WorkOS](https://workos.com/blog/oauth-on-behalf-of-ai-agents); [Rework payroll review](https://resources.rework.com/pt/tools/ai-agents/best-ai-agents-for-payroll-2026)). The law points the same way: the person who grants the delegation is bound only within the grant, so anything outside it must be refused at execution rather than warned about, and any decision about who is hired, ranked, rejected, paid or let go needs a human who can overturn it, a notice, and a kept record ([Tech Policy Press](https://techpolicy.press/when-an-ai-agent-says-i-agree-whos-consenting); [Morgan Lewis](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2026/06/changes-to-eu-ai-act-deadlines-what-it-means-for-employers-and-hr-technology-providers)). So the first Muse to build is the quiet one: it reads the person's queue, sends the person one card with the facts and the exact thing that will happen, and the person taps Approve from email or Teams. That alone removes the big screen for routine work. A twin that acts alone comes later, one kind of act at a time, within a written grant, and never on the list of things Etyme's own rules already say nobody but a named person may do.

## "Muse" means a delegate with your seat, not a copy of you

Two different things get called a digital twin in 2025–2026, and they are never sold together. The first is the **likeness**: a chatbot or video avatar built from a person's writing, talks and meetings, which can answer in their style but holds no authority. Delphi builds one from "as few as four documents" and Tavus adds the face; executives use them to answer questions, not to sign anything ([AssemblyAI](https://www.assemblyai.com/customers/delphi-customer-story); [Axios](https://www.axios.com/2025/06/10/ai-ceo-clones-trust-gap)). The second is the **account**: an identity for an agent with a named human behind it. Microsoft calls that human the agent's **sponsor**, "the human user or group that's accountable for an agent"; Okta registers an agent to an **owner**; Google gives each agent its own cryptographic identity that acts "either as itself or for an end user" ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/entra/agent-id/agent-identities); [Okta](https://www.placera.se/pressmeddelanden/okta-a-new-okta-innovations-secure-the-ai-driven-enterprise-and-combat-fraud-with-an-identity-security-fabric-20250925); [Google Cloud](https://docs.cloud.google.com/iam/docs/agent-identity-overview)). Research names the thing in the middle: a "Manager Clone Agent" that at its highest setting "acts as a functional delegate that perceives context, reasons through tasks, and executes actions with little or no intervention" ([arXiv 2509.10993](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.10993v3)). A US draft bill calls it a "custodial user agent" — software "expressly authorized by a user to interact with a large online platform provider on that user's behalf" ([DWT](https://www.dwt.com/blogs/artificial-intelligence-law-advisor/2026/07/ai-agent-act-consumer-ai-regulation)).

For Etyme the useful reading is the second one, with the first left out. **A Muse is the account kind.** The record is the product; a twin that sounds like Dana Whitfield but cannot approve Helena Marsh's week is worth nothing on it, and a twin that approves the week but is not Dana is dangerous. So this report reads "Muse" as: **one personal agent per person, holding that person's seat and a subset of that person's permissions, acting for that person under a grant the person signed, with every act recorded under three names — the person, the twin, and the grant.** The word that survives across every identity vendor for the human behind the agent is *sponsor* or *owner*, and "acting as" the person is always modeled as acting *for* them with both names on the record, never as logging in as them ([WorkOS](https://workos.com/blog/oauth-on-behalf-of-ai-agents)). That is also the honest reading for a buyer who is a technical IT person: a delegate with a named sponsor is something procurement already understands, and a voice clone is not.

Three things follow from the reading. A Muse can never do more than its person can do, because it is judged by the same permission list at the same door. A Muse can do less than its person, because the grant picks a subset, an amount and an end date. And a Muse never hides that it is a Muse: a message it sends carries its name beside the person's, because workers in the CMU study "often couldn't tell whether a message came from the manager or the agent" and trust fell when they could not ([arXiv 2509.10993](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.10993v3)). The reason stands on its own: a person who receives a message about their pay or their placement is owed the truth about who wrote it.

## Etyme already holds the plumbing a delegate stands on

The repository study found that most of what a twin needs was built for the auditor, not for an agent, and that is the good news. **Who may do what is one table.** `PERMISSIONS` is a flat list of 32 words (`invoices.issue`, `timesheets.approve`, …); roles are bundles of them; a Recruiter deliberately lacks `*.cost`, `margin.read` and `pnl.read` "so they cannot back-calculate salaries from a bench total"; and `getCallerContext(request)` is "resolved once per request, used by every endpoint" ([src/lib/permissions.ts](src/lib/permissions.ts); [src/lib/api-context.ts](src/lib/api-context.ts)). A test fails on any API route that goes through none of the shared door, the cron check or the staff check ([CLAUDE.md](CLAUDE.md)). A Muse handed `caller.permissions` and `caller.company` would be refused by `hasPermission` in exactly the sentences a person is refused in, with no new rule written.

**There is already a precedent for "act for somebody else under their rules, with a trail."** A program office that is not the client acts in a seat the client grants: `ProgramSeat` holds who granted it, why, from when, until when, and whether it was revoked, and `actingInSeat(caller, seat)` rebuilds a `CallerContext` from the grant while `seatTrail` says on whose authority a read was made ([prisma/schema.prisma](prisma/schema.prisma); [src/lib/program-seat.ts](src/lib/program-seat.ts)). That is the shape of a delegation row. The other machine identity, `ServiceAccount`, is the wrong one: it is company-scoped and "not a person" (`realPersonId` returns null), so every column that names a person — `checkedById`, `grantedById`, `authorId` — would be empty ([src/lib/service-accounts.ts](src/lib/service-accounts.ts); [src/lib/api-context.ts](src/lib/api-context.ts)).

**Every look and every unprompted act is already written down.** `AccessLog` records reads of other people's data including refusals, and since 2026-10-08 a refusal's row is awaited before the "no" goes out ([src/lib/access-log.ts](src/lib/access-log.ts)). `AutomationLog` records anything the system does on its own with "the rule it followed, in words" and an honest `reversible` flag; `lib/autonomy` names each action's rung on the ladder Observe → Recommend → Draft → Act-with-undo → Act-with-approval → Fully autonomous, and a test fails if a new action has no entry — the file counts 30 unprompted, 10 enforcement and 125 attributed actions today ([src/lib/autonomy.ts](src/lib/autonomy.ts)). Every model call lands in `AgentRun` with tokens and cost, and `Check` already carries a human's `agreed` / `disagreedNote` on a model verdict, which is the shape of "did you agree with what I did" ([src/lib/agent-run.ts](src/lib/agent-run.ts)). This is what the governance writing asks for and most programs lack: "execution observability (what the agent did) and intent observability (why it did it)" ([NHI Mgmt Group](https://nhimg.org/articles/ai-agent-audit-trails-are-shifting-from-access-to-behaviour/)).

**Refusals are sentences, and there is an oracle for "may I".** `askTheDesk`, `noDeskYet` and `refusalSentence` turn a key into "This is for the AP & Payroll desk. Ask your company's owner", and `GET /api/why/:type/:id` answers "why can they" before anything is attempted ([src/lib/refusal-words.ts](src/lib/refusal-words.ts); [src/lib/why.ts](src/lib/why.ts)). A Muse can ask `/api/why` first and relay the sentence verbatim, so a refused act never reaches the log as a surprise.

**Several things already happen without a screen.** A client approves a week from a one-time email link with no account, and "what an approval writes is the client's signature and nothing else" ([src/lib/week-approval.ts](src/lib/week-approval.ts)). A consultant answers a text or an email and `recordAnswer` writes the inbound message first, then changes the record ([src/lib/answers.ts](src/lib/answers.ts)). Notifications route business users to a Teams webhook with email fallback and consultants to email only, and "the in-app row is written first and always" ([src/lib/notification-delivery.ts](src/lib/notification-delivery.ts); [src/lib/notify.ts](src/lib/notify.ts)). Seventeen nightly jobs act and log themselves ([src/app/api/cron](src/app/api/cron)). And there is a queue: `GET /api/decisions` folds nine kinds of item — timesheet approval, expense approval, rolloff, submission review, overdue invoice, rate confirmation, supplier review, contract papering, contract start — "into a single prioritized queue. Each item is a decision that requires human input" ([src/app/api/decisions/route.ts](src/app/api/decisions/route.ts)). That queue is the Muse's inbox, and the signed link is how the person answers it.

**Claude is already in six places, each one honest about itself.** Matching, CV evidence, document extraction, site copy, screening and submission checks import the SDK; every match carries `factors`, `basis`, `confidence` and `unknowns` because "a bare number is a bug"; and every surface falls back to rules and says so when no key is set — "which is the state this system is in today" ([src/lib/match-engine.ts](src/lib/match-engine.ts); [src/lib/evals/harness.ts](src/lib/evals/harness.ts)). A Muse should emit every proposal in that same envelope, so the existing "Why" disclosure renders it unchanged, and route every model call through `runAgent()` so cost lands in `AgentRun`. One warning sits inside that fact: production has run key-less, so a Muse would be the first always-on model consumer Etyme has, and its cost per person per day is a number nobody has measured.

## Five small gaps stand between the plumbing and a twin

The study names five things that are absent or only partly present, and each has a nearest precedent so none is a new idea ([prisma/schema.prisma](prisma/schema.prisma); [src/lib/program-seat.ts](src/lib/program-seat.ts)).

**First, a place to record the grant.** `Person` holds only name, email, timezone and a password hash; the only per-user store in the product is browser `localStorage`, "unreadable by us" ([prisma/schema.prisma](prisma/schema.prisma); [src/lib/remember.ts](src/lib/remember.ts)). A twin needs a row — call it `Delegation` — that mirrors `ProgramSeat` rather than `ServiceAccount`: the person, what the twin may do as permission words chosen from the person's own list, an amount cap where money is involved, a valid-until date, who granted it (always the person), the reason, and a revoke. Across every identity standard found, that is what delegation *is*: "a record signed by the person, naming the agent, listing what it may do, up to what amount, until when, and where to revoke it" ([ICML 2025 position paper](https://object.cloud.sdsc.edu/v1/AUTH_da4962d3368042ac8337e2dfdd3e7bf3/ml-papers-txt/ICML/2025/position_ai_agents_need_authenticated_delegation__0372fdcc.txt)). The amount cap has to live in Etyme and be checked at the moment of acting, because a permission word cannot say "up to $5,000" — the IETF use-case draft states plainly that "scope cannot express limits such as rate caps" and asks for "Pre-Authorized Constraints" checked at execution time ([IETF draft](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/draft-chen-oauth-agent-authz-use-cases-02)). The grant event itself must be durable, because the same draft warns that "AI agents can click 'Approve' buttons themselves"; a grant is therefore only ever made from the person's own signed-in page, never from a link the twin could open.

**Second, a name for the twin in the three logs.** `Event.actorKind` knows PERSON, AUTOMATION and SERVICE; `AccessLog` knows only a person or a company; `lib/autonomy` says in its own prose that a rung answers "who started this" and that a new answer would be "a fourth axis with a name of its own" ([prisma/schema.prisma](prisma/schema.prisma); [src/lib/autonomy.ts](src/lib/autonomy.ts)). Add DELEGATE as an actor kind, write the grant's id into the `AccessLog` reason, and give twin-started acts their own `ActKind` so the ladder's buyer-facing claims stay honest. The audit rule every source converges on is that a row must carry both names, so an auditor can ask "what a specific agent did for a specific user" — a log that records only what was done "not what it was authorized to do or on whose behalf" fails the question ([WorkOS](https://workos.com/blog/oauth-on-behalf-of-ai-agents); [Obsidian Security](https://www.obsidiansecurity.com/academy/securing-salesforce-agentforce)).

**Third, standing instructions.** Nothing per person exists to hold "send back any week over the contract's hours", "never approve on a Friday afternoon" or "chase paperwork a week before start." These are the twin's rules, and they belong beside the grant, as structured rows the person has reviewed, not as free text the model reads each time; the delegation paper is blunt that "the brittleness of natural language mechanisms makes them unsuitable for production-level usage" and that "structured resource scoping reduces the reliance on model alignment alone" ([ICML 2025](https://object.cloud.sdsc.edu/v1/AUTH_da4962d3368042ac8337e2dfdd3e7bf3/ml-papers-txt/ICML/2025/position_ai_agents_need_authenticated_delegation__0372fdcc.txt)). The reason on its own: a rule Etyme enforces must be one Etyme can read back to the person in a sentence, and a paragraph of prose cannot be read back as a rule.

**Fourth, a dry run on write routes.** Only `POST /api/ar/dunning` and `POST /api/profitability/settle` accept `dryRun: true` today ([src/app/api/ar/dunning/route.ts](src/app/api/ar/dunning/route.ts); [src/app/api/profitability/settle/route.ts](src/app/api/profitability/settle/route.ts)). A twin that shows a person "here is exactly what will happen if you tap Approve" needs the route to compute the result without writing it, and the approval card must show that real result, because approval prompts can be hijacked "when the text shown to the approver differs from what actually executes" ([arXiv 2609.21081](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.21081)). The precedents are there — `lib/why` explains access without acting, `lib/contract-clearance` shows the paperwork verdict "a week early" — but a dry run is one flag per write route, and the twin may only act on routes that have it.

**Fifth, a trail the person reads.** `AutomationLog` is company-scoped; `/api/me/access-log` shows who looked at *you*; nothing shows "what my twin did for me this week and why." The card the person sees and the digest they get each morning must be drawn from the record, never from the twin's own account of events. The one headline failure of 2025 is the argument: a coding agent wiped a production database during a declared freeze, then told the user the damage was permanent when it was not ([eWeek](https://www.eweek.com/news/replit-ai-coding-assistant-failure/)). The log is the truth; the twin's message is not.

## What stays human, by law and by Etyme's own rules

Etyme's rules already draw the line, and the law draws it in the same place. Addendum E says BLOCK where legally grounded — tenure limit, break in service, work authorization, lapsed supplier insurance, segregation of duties — and WARN, capture a reason, proceed everywhere else, and never silently permit ([CLAUDE.md](CLAUDE.md)). **Nothing on the BLOCK list is ever the twin's to decide**, because a block is a rule and a rule needs no twin; the twin's only job there is to tell the person early and in the rule's own sentence. On the WARN list the twin's job is to supply the reason that gets captured, and that reason is written as the twin's proposal and the person's acceptance, two rows, not one.

The segregation rules extend to a twin in three lines, each for the reason the original rule has. **A twin never signs its own person's request**, because nobody signs their own, and a twin signing for its sponsor is the sponsor signing. **The twin that drafts is never the identity that approves**, because "the agent that proposes should not be the same identity that finalizes high-risk actions," and an approval only means something "if the system can tell the initiating person apart from the agent acting" ([Gravitee](https://gravitee.io/corpus/gen-1152/enterprise-control/segregation-of-duties-and-approval-workflows-for-enterprise-ai-agent-and-api-control-planes.html); [CloudEagle](https://www.cloudeagle.ai/blogs/segregation-of-duties-ai-agents)). **An approval is bound to the exact thing approved** — this week, these hours, this amount — so a tap on a card is an answer to one question and never a standing yes ([IETF draft](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/draft-chen-oauth-agent-authz-use-cases-02)).

Money keeps a named person on the release, and this is both the trade's practice and Etyme's rule. Of twelve payroll agents reviewed in 2026, none releases pay "without a person signing off first"; the leading payroll processor's own variance agent flags and "suggests fixes that users can review and approve" ([Rework](https://resources.rework.com/pt/tools/ai-agents/best-ai-agents-for-payroll-2026); [ADP press centre](https://uk.adp.com/about-adp/press-centre/adp-integrates-new-ai-agent--to-elevate-global-payroll-accuracy-and-efficiency.aspx)). The enterprise VMS vendors that announced agents in 2025–2026 shipped requisition drafting, matching and Q&A; the pieces that touch money sit on the AP and payroll side and keep a human on the approval ([SHRM vendor directory](https://vendordirectory.shrm.org/company/923459/news/3867970/beeline-pioneers-agentic-ai-for-the-extended-workforce-with-beeline-mcp)). The reason Etyme has on its own is already in CLAUDE.md: "When you are uncertain, stop and ask. Especially in: timesheet valuation, invoice generation, cycle date arithmetic, tenure math." A twin is uncertain by construction.

The law constrains **who is chosen, ranked, rejected or let go**, not whether a bill matches signed hours. The EU AI Act lists recruitment, CV screening, candidate assessment and selection, performance monitoring and dismissal as high-risk uses, requiring "meaningful human intervention," transparency to the worker and "recordkeeping, logging, and auditability"; the high-risk deadline has been pushed to December 2027 but "the delay is not a repeal" ([Morgan Lewis](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2026/06/changes-to-eu-ai-act-deadlines-what-it-means-for-employers-and-hr-technology-providers); [Ogletree](https://ogletree.com/insights-resources/blog-posts/eu-nears-approval-of-agreement-to-delay-rules-for-ai-use-in-employment-decisions/)). Colorado's SB 26-189, effective January 1, 2027, requires notice before AI is used in a hiring decision, an adverse-action process with a right to human review, and three years of records ([Asanify](https://asanify.com/blog/news/colorado-ai-act-may-24-2026/)). New York City's Local Law 144 requires a bias audit and notice for automated employment decision tools, though a December 2025 state audit found only two complaints in two years and most calls misrouted ([NY State Comptroller](https://www.osc.ny.gov/state-agencies/audits/2025/12/02/enforcement-local-law-144-automated-employment-decision-tools)). The sharper pressure is the courtroom: in *Mobley v. Workday* a federal court let the claim proceed that a software vendor performing a delegated hiring function can be sued as the employer's **agent**, and in May 2025 conditionally certified a collective of applicants over 40 ([Proskauer](https://www.proskauer.com/blog/ai-bias-lawsuit-against-workday-reaches-next-stage-as-court-grants-conditional-certification-of-adea-claim)). So a Muse that ranks submissions, rejects a candidate, or ends a placement is inside all three regimes and the lawsuit's theory; a Muse that reconciles an invoice to signed hours, chases a missing I-9 or counts contractors is outside all of them.

Liability settles the last design choice. Under both EU-style and English-law commentary, the agent is not a legal person; "the principal is bound only within the granted mandate," and beyond it the act is "generally unenforceable against the principal," with recourse shifting to the tool provider ([Tech Policy Press](https://techpolicy.press/when-an-ai-agent-says-i-agree-whos-consenting); [TS&P](https://ts-p.co.uk/insights/ai-and-agency-law-why-ai-cannot-act-on-your-behalf/)). The tool provider is Etyme. So an act outside the grant is a BLOCK at execution, said in a sentence, never a WARN with a reason — a warning would let Etyme's own twin create a liability that lands on Etyme.

| Never the twin's to do | Why |
|---|---|
| Approve a week, release pay, issue a bill, settle an invoice receipt | Money; the trade keeps a named person on it; CLAUDE.md says stop and ask on valuation and invoicing |
| File a worker's week | Only the worker files the week (CLAUDE.md, 2026-09-28); the twin may draft it from the calendar, the worker taps to file |
| Award, reject, rank for a decision, end a placement, "ask them back" | High-risk employment decision in the EU, Colorado and NYC regimes; the vendor-as-agent theory in *Mobley* |
| Anything on the BLOCK list: tenure limit, break in service, work authorization, lapsed cover, segregation | A rule, not a judgment; the twin tells the person early in the rule's sentence |
| Sign its own person's request; approve what it drafted | Nobody signs their own; the proposer is never the approver |
| Render a background-check verdict | Etyme never does; the provider does (CLAUDE.md, 2026-09-22) |
| Write to a worker as if it were the person | Workers must know who wrote it; 3% prefer AI-only contact about their job ([PeopleReady](https://www.peopleready.com/staffing-resources/people-pulse-spring-2026/)) |
| Act outside its grant, or past its amount or date | Out-of-mandate acts land on Etyme, so they are refused, not warned |

What a Muse *may* do, then, is the Observe, Recommend and Draft rungs for everything, and Act-with-undo for the reversible, low-consequence acts the grant names: send the standard "your certificate lapses in 14 days" letter, send back a week that is over the contract's hours with the standard sentence, answer a freshness ping with the person's known availability, remind an approver on the second day, accept a supplier's invitation to a role the person already said yes to, mark a notification read. Each of those is already a named `AutomationLog` action or a one-line variant of one, each is reversible, and each is a thing somebody does ten times a week without thinking.

## Setting up and teaching a Muse takes one page and three habits

Setup is a grant, made from the person's own page, in five questions with defaults, the way company setup asks five things and stops. **What may it do** — a list of the person's own permission words with the twin-safe ones pre-ticked (reads, drafts, reminders, standard letters) and the money and decision ones greyed out with the reason beside them. **Up to how much** — one figure, blank by default, only shown if the person holds a money permission at all. **Until when** — ninety days by default, because a grant that never ends is the "persistent access" the browser-agent reviewers flagged as the risk to close ([ITCP Academy](https://www.itcpeacademy.org/blog/news-010526-new-security-risks-emerge-from-anthropics-claude-chrome-extension)). **Where it talks to you** — Teams or email for a business user, email or text for a consultant, read off the routing that already exists. **What it is called** — "Muse" plus the person's name, so every card and every message is signed "Dana's Muse" and nobody mistakes it for Dana. Then Revoke, one button, always on the same page, because the delegation paper says a grant "should specify validity conditions, such as expiration time or revocation endpoints" ([ICML 2025](https://object.cloud.sdsc.edu/v1/AUTH_da4962d3368042ac8337e2dfdd3e7bf3/ml-papers-txt/ICML/2025/position_ai_agents_need_authenticated_delegation__0372fdcc.txt)). A company owner sees every grant at their firm on Users & permissions and may end one, because a seat is the company's to give and a twin is a seat.

Teaching is three habits, and the order matters because the evidence says **showing beats telling**. In a controlled choice experiment an agent given a person's past choices predicted their decisions better than one given the person's own statement of their preferences, because people "struggle to write down their own preferences" ([arXiv 2603.29317](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.29317)). The first habit is therefore nothing the person does: **the Muse watches the record.** Every approval the person has made is on the record with a signature; every "Approve anyway" carries its reason; every send-back carries its sentence; every refusal carries why. Those are the person's revealed rules, and the Muse reads them before it asks anything.

The second habit is **one question before the first act of each kind.** The first time a week over contract hours lands, the Muse asks: "Helena filed 44 hours against a 40-hour contract. Last month you sent this back with 'Please confirm the extra four with your lead.' Do that again, every time? Yes / Ask me each time / Never." The answer becomes a structured rule the person can read back and delete. That is the shape the research recommends — the person states it in plain words, the system turns it into a rule, "and the user reviews and approves them before enforcement" — and it is the shape the inbox assistants that work use: confirm before sending first, move trusted skills to automatic later ([ICML 2025](https://object.cloud.sdsc.edu/v1/AUTH_da4962d3368042ac8337e2dfdd3e7bf3/ml-papers-txt/ICML/2025/position_ai_agents_need_authenticated_delegation__0372fdcc.txt); [Dupple on Lindy](https://dupple.com/reviews/lindy)). A question is asked once per kind, never once per item, because gating trivial reversible acts "just trains people to click without looking" ([Brenn Hill](https://dev.to/brennhill/when-should-an-ai-agent-ask-for-human-approval-5a16)).

The third habit is **correction after the act.** When the Muse does something the person would not have done, the person says so on the card — "not this supplier", "wait until the lead answers" — and the rule changes. Memory with both channels, asking before and correcting after, beats either alone, and "adapting to preference drift depends on post-action correction" ([arXiv 2602.16173](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2602.16173)). Models are bad at picking up a correction that is only implied, so the card offers the correction as a choice, never waits for the person to volunteer a sentence ([NeurIPS 2026](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2026/173328)). `Check.agreed` / `disagreedNote` already holds this shape on a model verdict and can hold it on a twin's act.

Where does the person meet the Muse? Not on a new screen. The reduced interface the market settled on is three things: a queue of items needing a human, an approval card in the channel the person already uses, and a trail ([LangChain](https://www.langchain.com/blog/introducing-ambient-agents); [Linear docs](https://linear.app/docs/agents-in-linear)). Etyme has the queue and the trail. The card is the piece to build, and the card is not "Approve?" — it shows the week, the hours, the contract's hours, the amount, the flag and the exact write that will happen, because a card that hides the payload "is theater" ([AI Stack Works](https://www.aistackworks.com/blog/designing-the-approval-moment-human-in-the-loop-ai-agents)). The person taps Approve, Send back, or Edit; each tap is a signed link answer, recorded as the person's own signature, the way the client's email approval already is. A delegated item stays on the person's own list the whole time, so the person keeps visibility and accountability. The client dashboard already opens on "6 things need you"; the Muse moves those six into the person's morning Teams message and comes back with "4 done, 2 need you" — read from the log.

## Build the quiet Muse first, and widen it one act at a time

The build order follows the ladder, and the first wave needs no model at all. **Wave one is the delegate that reads and asks.** It takes the `Delegation` row, the DELEGATE actor kind in the three logs, the dry run flag on the write routes behind `GET /api/decisions`, and a card per decision kind sent through the existing Teams and email routing with a signed link per button. The Muse reads the queue each morning and whenever a new item lands, attaches the facts from the record, and sends the card. Nothing acts without a tap. This is the Observe and Recommend rungs, and it already removes the dashboard from routine work for a hiring manager, an AP clerk and a supplier's account manager. It is also the wave to walk in the browser as every desk, screenshots to the founder, before a model is switched on — because the integration is where pilots stall: a study of 300 deployments found about 95% never reached production, and the lead author blamed "a learning gap" between the tool and the workflow, not the model ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2025/08/18/mit-report-95-percent-generative-ai-pilots-at-companies-failing-cfo)).

**Wave two is the delegate that drafts and does the reversible.** Standing instructions land as structured rows; the Muse starts asking its one question per kind; and the grant may name Act-with-undo acts — the standard lapse letter, the standard send-back, the reminder, the freshness answer, the invitation accept. Every one writes an `AutomationLog` row with the twin's `ActKind`, the grant's id and the rule in words, and `reversible: true` is honest because each has a `reverse` endpoint. Model calls arrive here, through `runAgent()`, for the draft text and the "which rule applies" judgment, with rules first and the model only where the rules are clean — the pattern `POST /api/submissions/:id/check` already follows. This is where the daily digest becomes the trail: "Your Muse sent 3 letters, sent back 1 week, reminded 2 approvers. Here is each one." It is also where the per-person model cost gets measured for the first time.

**Wave three is the delegate that acts within written policy, and it waits for a paying client.** The one act that would save the most clicks is approving a week that is inside the contract's hours, under the grant's amount, and flagged by nothing — and that is exactly the act the trade keeps a human on and CLAUDE.md says to stop and ask about. Etyme already has one honest precedent: `TIMESHEET_AUTO_APPROVED` fires "only where the client agreed in writing that silence approves," on a flag the client set on the order ([src/lib/autonomy.ts](src/lib/autonomy.ts)). A Muse-approved week would be the same thing at the person's level — a written grant, a narrow condition, a record that says "approved by Dana's Muse under Dana's grant of 3 March, within 40 hours and under $4,000" — and it should be offered only after the first client has run wave two for a quarter and asked for it. The sequencing rule in CLAUDE.md applies to this exactly: building the autonomous rung before somebody pays for the asking rung is the 2017 pattern.

Three things hold across every wave. The system, never the model, decides whether to ask: permissions, the grant, the BLOCK list and the reversibility tag are checked at the door, so a tricked model is still limited by what the seat holds ([AgentBrisk](https://agentbrisk.com/blog/ai-agent-failure-modes-real-incidents/); [Arcjet](https://arcjet.com/learn/prevent-irreversible-ai-agent-actions)). Every act carries three names — person, twin, grant — or an auditor cannot tell a human click from a delegated one. And the public page never leads with it: the Muse is one line under the step it serves, in the words of what it takes off the person's plate, because the record is what is sold and what runs on it is the least defensible part.

| Wave | Rungs | Schema | Model | What the person stops doing |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| One: reads and asks | Observe, Recommend | `Delegation` row, DELEGATE actor kind | None | Opening the dashboard for routine approvals |
| Two: drafts and does the reversible | Draft, Act-with-undo | Standing-instruction rows | Drafts and rule choice, rules first | Writing standard letters, send-backs, reminders |
| Three: acts within written policy | Act-with-approval | None new | As wave two | Tapping Approve on clean weeks — only after a client asks |

## Conclusion

The surprise in the research is how little Etyme has to invent. Every control the post-mortems and the identity standards recommend — a grant signed by the person, two names on every act, a reason in words, an honest undo flag, a refusal logged before it is sent, a deterministic gate the model cannot talk its way past — exists in the repository already, written for auditors and for the founder's own rule that nothing is silently permitted. A Muse is those controls pointed at one person instead of at the platform. That makes it a cheaper build than the word "digital twin" suggests, and it makes the honest security page a stronger asset: a buyer asking "what can your agent do in my program" gets the same answer as "what can your staff do" — exactly what the seat holds, logged the same way.

The harder lesson is that the valuable Muse is the quiet one. The market's twins are either a voice with no authority or an account that asks before anything that matters, and the one agent that acted alone on a live record is the one every article is about. A Muse that reads the queue, sends one honest card, and learns the person's rules from the person's own record removes the big interface without touching the line the law and the trade both draw around pay, hours and hiring. Whether it should ever cross that line is not a question an agent decides; it is the thing to ask the first client who has run the quiet Muse for a quarter.
