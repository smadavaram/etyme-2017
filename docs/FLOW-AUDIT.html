Adversarial review of my own design

Where the loops break

I traced every flow in the build spec looking for loops that don't close. Eighteen are broken.

Six of them are in sign-up — the first loop anyone actually touches — and I had missed that flow entirely until you asked. One of those six carries legal exposure.

1

What "broken" means for a loop

A loop is broken when it cannot run a second time and come out better. Four ways that happens:

It never closes

The feedback arrow is drawn but the data never actually arrives.

It has a dead end

Work reaches a human or a final state and nothing goes back.

It collects rubbish

No exit rule, so bad records accumulate and poison the numbers.

It's a hope, not a mechanism

The picture shows improvement. Nothing in the design causes it.

The fourth one is the dangerous kind — and it is the one I got wrong in the most expensive place.

A drawn arrow feels like a solved problem. It isn't until something specific, scheduled and owned makes it move.

2

The sign-up loop, which I never traced

Everything else in this audit assumes a vendor is already using the product. Nothing in the design gets them there.

The homepage promises: "Upload ten CVs. See them scored against a live requirement within the hour." Follow that promise backwards and there is no machinery behind it at all.

Break 02 is not a design problem, it is a legal one.

In the US, texting individuals without prior express consent carries statutory damages per message. Fifty consultants times a fortnightly ping is a real number, and "the vendor said it was fine" is not obviously a defence.

Have a lawyer look at this before the first message goes out — not before launch, before the first message. It is the only item in this whole audit I would stop for.

How to close it

The fix is not to build a self-serve onboarding. It is to admit the first ten vendors are done by hand, and make the hand-work fast.

Fixes 01 and 06

Ask for the requirement on the form

Add one field: "Paste a requirement you're working on right now." They have one open in another tab. It costs them ten seconds and it hands you the exact thing the promise needs — a live requirement to score their bench against.

And drop the login. Send the scored shortlist back as a link or a PDF. Do not make them create an account to see whether you are any good. The account comes after they say "that's useful", never before.

Fixes 02

The first text is an opt-in, not a ping

Message one identifies the vendor by name, says why they are hearing from you, and asks permission to continue. STOP works from that first message. Freshness pings only start after a yes, and consent is stored per person with a timestamp and a source.

And the vendor confirms at upload that they have a relationship with each person. Put it in the upload step and in the contract. Then get it reviewed.

Fixes 07

A named person and a clock

A form submission with no owner dies at 9pm on a Friday. One named person, a two-hour reply target during working hours, and two follow-up attempts before it is closed with a reason.

The reason is the loop. "Too small", "no bench", "went quiet" tells you who to approach next. Without it, a dead lead teaches you nothing.

Fixes 08

You are the prime vendor — say so, then act like one

Supplying your own consultants while also running the platform is the master vendor model. It is completely normal in staffing and your old schema already models it. The risk is not the client objecting — it is sub-vendors quietly leaving.

Label your own submissions in the client's inbox, put the arrangement in the contract, and then earn the supply side with the rules below. Undisclosed it is fatal; disclosed and fairly run it is the whole business.

Fixes 09

Free needs a start date and an end date

"Free until five a day" has no day zero and no defined finish. Both sides will remember it differently, and that conversation happens exactly when you are asking for money.

Write it down at sign-up: the clock starts on their first uploaded bench, ends at five good submissions a day or ninety days, whichever comes first, and here is the dashboard you will both be reading.

Worth noting

Nobody checks who is signing up

Anyone can claim to be a staffing firm. Harmless while the vendor side only shows their own bench — but the moment client requirements are visible, a competitor signing up sees real demand.

Gate the requirement feed, not the sign-up. Let anyone in to see their own bench scored; require a conversation with a person before they see anything a client posted.

Being the prime vendor without killing your own supply

You said you want others supplying alongside you. That is the right instinct — your own bench caps your revenue at the size of your bench, and sub-vendors uncap it. A smaller spread on unlimited coverage beats a full spread on fifty people.

But the participation loop is fragile, and it dies from the supply side, not the client side.

Why this is worth the discipline.

On your own consultant you bill $85, pay $60 and keep $25. On a sub-vendor placement you might keep $10 — but with no bench to carry, no idle days, and no ceiling on coverage.

The blended business beats either alone, which means you should genuinely want sub-vendors to win sometimes. If that sentence feels wrong, the model will not hold.

5

The worst one: improvement was never designed

The business plan says the agent share goes from 30% to 88% over four years. That single line carries the entire margin story — 45% gross margin becomes 84% because of it.

Nothing in the design makes it happen.

A drawn arrow is not a mechanism. This one is worth four points of margin a year.

6

The metric can't tell you what's wrong

I told you to watch one number: good submissions per day, per requirement. Target five. That advice is right and the instrumentation behind it is useless.

If it stalls at two, five completely different things could be causing it — and each needs an opposite fix.

This is the cheapest fix on the page.

It is six counters on data you are already storing. Build it in week one and month three becomes a diagnosis instead of a guess.

7

The other nine, with fixes

Break 10 · serious

Escalation is a dead end

Three failed checks send it to a person. They fix it by hand and move on. Nothing records why it failed, so the identical failure arrives again next week — forever.

Fix: Every escalation must close with a cause from a short list. Causes aggregate. When one hits ten, it becomes a work item against the agent. That is the improvement loop from section 5, fed automatically.

Break 11 · serious

Ghosts never leave the bench

Two pings, no reply, marked unconfirmed — and then nothing. After a year a large share of the bench is people who left the market, still counted in the idle-days number on the front screen.

Fix: Unconfirmed for 30 days raises one call task for a recruiter. Still nothing at 60 days, archive automatically and drop them from every count. One click restores them.

Break 12 · serious

Requirements change and nothing notices

Rates move, locations change, roles get put on hold. Every flow assumes the requirement is frozen. Twenty scored submissions silently become wrong, and cancelled roles keep burning compute.

Fix: Version the requirement. Stamp every score with the version it was scored against. On change, re-score what is in flight and flag stale shortlists. On cancel, stop everything downstream immediately.

Break 13 · serious

The quality check asks the wrong person

"Rate below market" is shown to whoever typed the requirement — usually a coordinator with no authority over pay. They cannot fix it, so they override. Within a month overriding is automatic and the check is theatre.

Fix: Route each problem to whoever can act on it — rate goes to the hiring manager with the market data attached. And watch the override rate: above half, the check is failing, not the user.

Break 14 · serious

Ten a week, but which ten?

Left to a person, they will review the easy ones. Left to "most recent", you will never see the rare failures. Either way the agreement rate looks great and means nothing.

Fix: Assign the sample, never let it be chosen. Mix it: some random, some from the 70–80 score band where errors cluster, some shortlisted, and every case where the rules and the model disagreed.

Break 15 · fixable

Nobody designed the word "no"

The consent text asks yes or no. The spec only says what happens on yes. A no leaves the submission in limbo and throws away the most valuable reply of all — "someone already put me forward there".

Fix: No cancels the submission, tells the recruiter, and asks one follow-up: already submitted, rate too low, or not interested. That third answer is free deduplication and free market data.

Break 16 · fixable

Dedup never learns

A person judges "not the same person" in the check queue. The verdict corrects that one record and vanishes. The rule that made the mistake is unchanged, so it makes it again.

Fix: Same shape as break 10 — the human verdict is training data, not just a correction. Feed disagreements back into the matching thresholds monthly.

Break 17 · fixable

The benchmark judges itself

Market rates are built from your own submissions, then used to tell clients their rate is below market. Early on your bench is the sample, so an expensive bench makes every client look underpriced.

Fix: Do not use the benchmark below a minimum sample per skill and city, and show the sample size beside every figure. Sanity-check the first year against published survey data.

Break 18 · known limit

The check cannot catch a liar

It confirms the CV says seven years of Spring Boot. It cannot confirm the seven years happened. A well-written fake passes every check cleanly.

Fix: None available, and pretending otherwise is worse than admitting it. The check catches sloppiness; interviews catch lying. Never sell it as fraud detection — that promise is the one that ends the relationship.

8

Fix these five before any code

The other thirteen can be fixed as you go. These five change what you build — and the first two change what you do this week.

01

Get the SMS consent question in front of a lawyer

Before a single message goes out, not before launch. Everything else on this page is a design decision you can revise. This one has statutory damages attached and no amount of good intent undoes a send.

02

Make the first hour real, by hand

Add the requirement field to the form, name one person to reply within two hours, and send the scored shortlist back without asking anyone to create an account. Ten vendors done manually beats a self-serve flow nobody completes.

03

Put a name and a Friday on the improvement loop

One person, one hour a week, one change, measured against a frozen eval set, kept or reverted on the number. Without this the margin curve in the business plan is decoration and year four looks like year one.

04

Assume outcomes will not arrive, and infer them

Treat silence as a label, not missing data. "Not viewed in 14 days" and "requirement closed with nobody hired" are both real signals. Retrofitting inferred outcomes into a schema that assumed clean feedback is a rewrite.

05

Build the funnel, not the number

Six counters instead of one. A day of work on data you already have, and the difference between diagnosing month three and guessing at it.

What this review does not change.

The shape is still right. The submission check loop, the screening check loop and the human sample are sound, and they are the parts that make this different from an ATS.

What is wrong is that I drew several arrows as though drawing them made them work, and never traced the one flow every user hits first. Six of the eighteen breaks share one mistake: a loop that ends at a human and stops there. Every one of those needs the human's verdict to become an input.
