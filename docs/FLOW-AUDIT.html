Adversarial review of my own design

Where the loops break

I traced every flow in the build spec looking for loops that don't close. Twelve are broken. Three of them badly enough to matter before anyone writes code.

1

What "broken" means for a loop

A loop is broken when it cannot run a second time and come out better. Four ways that happens:

It never closes

The feedback arrow is drawn but the data never actually arrives.

It has a dead end

Work reaches a human or a final state and nothing goes back.

It collects rubbish

No exit rule, so bad records accumulate and poison the numbers.

It's a hope, not a mechanism

The picture shows improvement. Nothing in the design causes it.

The fourth one is the dangerous kind — and it is the one I got wrong in the most expensive place.

A drawn arrow feels like a solved problem. It isn't until something specific, scheduled and owned makes it move.

4

The worst one: improvement was never designed

The business plan says the agent share goes from 30% to 88% over four years. That single line carries the entire margin story — 45% gross margin becomes 84% because of it.

Nothing in the design makes it happen.

A drawn arrow is not a mechanism. This one is worth four points of margin a year.

5

The metric can't tell you what's wrong

I told you to watch one number: good submissions per day, per requirement. Target five. That advice is right and the instrumentation behind it is useless.

If it stalls at two, five completely different things could be causing it — and each needs an opposite fix.

This is the cheapest fix on the page.

It is six counters on data you are already storing. Build it in week one and month three becomes a diagnosis instead of a guess.

6

The other nine, with fixes

Break 04 · serious

Escalation is a dead end

Three failed checks send it to a person. They fix it by hand and move on. Nothing records why it failed, so the identical failure arrives again next week — forever.

Fix: Every escalation must close with a cause from a short list. Causes aggregate. When one hits ten, it becomes a work item against the agent. That is the improvement loop from section 4, fed automatically.

Break 05 · serious

Ghosts never leave the bench

Two pings, no reply, marked unconfirmed — and then nothing. After a year a large share of the bench is people who left the market, still counted in the idle-days number on the front screen.

Fix: Unconfirmed for 30 days raises one call task for a recruiter. Still nothing at 60 days, archive automatically and drop them from every count. One click restores them.

Break 06 · serious

Requirements change and nothing notices

Rates move, locations change, roles get put on hold. Every flow assumes the requirement is frozen. Twenty scored submissions silently become wrong, and cancelled roles keep burning compute.

Fix: Version the requirement. Stamp every score with the version it was scored against. On change, re-score what is in flight and flag stale shortlists. On cancel, stop everything downstream immediately.

Break 07 · serious

The quality check asks the wrong person

"Rate below market" is shown to whoever typed the requirement — usually a coordinator with no authority over pay. They cannot fix it, so they override. Within a month overriding is automatic and the check is theatre.

Fix: Route each problem to whoever can act on it — rate goes to the hiring manager with the market data attached. And watch the override rate: above half, the check is failing, not the user.

Break 08 · serious

Ten a week, but which ten?

Left to a person, they will review the easy ones. Left to "most recent", you will never see the rare failures. Either way the agreement rate looks great and means nothing.

Fix: Assign the sample, never let it be chosen. Mix it: some random, some from the 70–80 score band where errors cluster, some shortlisted, and every case where the rules and the model disagreed.

Break 09 · fixable

Nobody designed the word "no"

The consent text asks yes or no. The spec only says what happens on yes. A no leaves the submission in limbo and throws away the most valuable reply of all — "someone already put me forward there".

Fix: No cancels the submission, tells the recruiter, and asks one follow-up: already submitted, rate too low, or not interested. That third answer is free deduplication and free market data.

Break 10 · fixable

Dedup never learns

A person judges "not the same person" in the check queue. The verdict corrects that one record and vanishes. The rule that made the mistake is unchanged, so it makes it again.

Fix: Same shape as break 04 — the human verdict is training data, not just a correction. Feed disagreements back into the matching thresholds monthly.

Break 11 · fixable

The benchmark judges itself

Market rates are built from your own submissions, then used to tell clients their rate is below market. Early on your bench is the sample, so an expensive bench makes every client look underpriced.

Fix: Do not use the benchmark below a minimum sample per skill and city, and show the sample size beside every figure. Sanity-check the first year against published survey data.

Break 12 · known limit

The check cannot catch a liar

It confirms the CV says seven years of Spring Boot. It cannot confirm the seven years happened. A well-written fake passes every check cleanly.

Fix: None available, and pretending otherwise is worse than admitting it. The check catches sloppiness; interviews catch lying. Never sell it as fraud detection — that promise is the one that ends the relationship.

7

Fix these three before any code

The other nine can be fixed as you go. These three change what you build.

01

Put a name and a Friday on the improvement loop

One person, one hour a week, one change, measured against a frozen eval set, kept or reverted on the number. Without this the margin curve in the business plan is decoration and year four looks like year one.

02

Assume outcomes will not arrive, and infer them

Treat silence as a label, not missing data. "Not viewed in 14 days" and "requirement closed with nobody hired" are both real signals. Design for them now — retrofitting inferred outcomes into a schema that assumed clean feedback is a rewrite.

03

Build the funnel, not the number

Six counters instead of one. It is a day of work on data you already have, and it is the difference between diagnosing month three and guessing at it.

What this review does not change.

The shape is still right. The submission check loop, the screening check loop and the human sample are sound, and they are the parts that make this different from an ATS.

What is wrong is that I drew several arrows as though drawing them made them work. Six of the twelve breaks are the same mistake: a loop that ends at a human and stops there. Every one of those needs the human's verdict to become an input.
