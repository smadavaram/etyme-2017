Follow-up · who pays, and the people-plus-agents version

The hybrid crew

You asked whether you'd be Stripe or an MSP, and who actually writes the cheque. Short version: neither, not yet — and the mixed crew of people and agents is the better business anyway.

The short answer

For the first three years you are a staffing firm. You get paid exactly the way your old customers got paid: a company hands you work, you deliver it, you invoice.

Stripe is a rail. Rails need enormous volume before they earn anything, and Stripe itself didn't start as one — it started as the easiest way for a single developer to take a card. The rail came later. Same here. Volume first, rail later.

01

Four ways to get paid, in the order you can have them

Each one is only possible because of the one before it. You cannot skip a rung — every company that tried to start at the top died waiting for volume that never came.

01
now → year 3

You are a staffing firm

A company sends you a pile of work. You get it done with a crew of agents and people. You charge per finished piece of work. They never see your software.

Who pays The company, from money it already spends on an outsourcing vendor. You take 40–80% gross margin.
02
year 1 → 3

You are the record-keeper

Companies already running their own agents have no idea what they cost or whether they work. You sell them the ledger and the scorecard — the thing you built for yourself in rung 1.

Who pays The same company, from its software budget. A subscription plus a small fee per piece of work measured.
03
year 3 → 6

You are the MSP

Other people's agents get listed, tested and hired through you. You run the marketplace and take a cut of everything hired through it.

Who pays Companies pay 3–8% of what they spend on agents. Agent builders pay a small fee to be verified.
04
year 6 →

You are the rail — the Stripe answer

Everyone settles agent work through you because you set the standard for what "finished" means. You also insure the outcome and finance the gap between paying your suppliers fast and getting paid slow.

Who pays Everyone, a thin slice. Plus insurance premiums, which is where the real money is by then.
Rung 4 is worth the most and is worth nothing without rungs 1 to 3. Sell services now; the rail is built out of the receipts.
02

Who literally signs the cheque

This is the part most AI companies get wrong, and it decides whether you have a business in twelve months.

You are not asking anyone for new budget. You are taking over a line that already exists — the money a company already pays an outsourcing firm or a temp agency to get boring work done. That budget has a number attached, a cost per transaction, and a person whose job is to make it smaller.

Sell toWhy
VP of Operations Owns the outsourcing contract. Measured on cost per transaction. Will switch vendors for a better number and an SLA.
Head of Shared Services Runs the back office. Already buys work by the unit. Understands your pricing immediately.
Controller / Revenue Cycle lead Drowning in exceptions, has overtime spend they'd love to kill.
Not the CTO Buys pilots. Pilots die. No recurring budget line, no operational pain of their own.
Not the Head of AI Buys experiments and platforms, is measured on innovation rather than cost, and their budget vanishes in a downturn.
Your warmest first customer

Staffing companies themselves — the people who bought Etyme. They have huge volumes of onboarding paperwork, timesheet chasing, compliance checks and invoice exceptions. They know you. They are frightened about AI and have no idea what to do. And you already speak their language fluently, which no competitor does.

03

The hybrid crew, as a loop

Yes — and it's stronger than the pure-agent version. Here's the whole thing in one picture, then in words.

No real business is 100% automatable today. Agents get somewhere between 40% and 80% of real work right first time. So a person has to be in the loop whether you sell it that way or not. Once you accept that, you may as well sell the whole crew — people and agents together, one price, one promise.

The same thing in words

  • Work arrives. An invoice to reconcile, a document to check, a claim to review.
  • You route it. Work the agent handles well goes to the agent. The rest goes to a person. You decide the split, not the client.
  • It gets checked. If it fails, the agent tries again. If it keeps failing, a person finishes it.
  • You bill for finished work. One price, whoever did it. The client doesn't know the mix and doesn't care.
  • A person spot-checks a sample of what came out, so you find out when the agent is quietly wrong.
  • A person fixes the cause and adds that case to the test set — so the same mistake can't happen twice.
  • The split moves. More work goes to the agent next month. Your price stays the same. Your cost drops.
The client buys a finished job. You quietly change who does it. That gap is the entire business.
04

People do three different jobs here

This is the part worth getting right. "Human in the loop" is usually one vague idea. It's actually three separate jobs at three points in the loop, and they behave completely differently over time.

The Doer

shrinks fastest

Does the work the agent couldn't. Expensive — this is a full unit of human labour. Year one this is most of your cost. By year four it's a small team handling genuinely hard cases.

The Checker

shrinks slowly

Looks at a sample of finished work to catch quiet mistakes. Cheap, because checking is faster than doing. Never goes to zero — this is the job that keeps you honest.

The Coach

stays or grows

Turns failures into fixes and test cases. Your highest-value people. This is the job that shrinks the other two, so you want more of it, not less.

Over four years the same headcount covers many times the volume, because your people move up: mostly Doers at the start, mostly Coaches by the end. Nobody gets fired; the work changes shape. That is also, incidentally, the honest version of this story to tell a customer.

Do not skip this

The Checker must be a person, and the Checker must not be the same agent that did the work. An agent checking its own work agrees with itself. You will get a beautiful dashboard saying 96% correct while the client's complaints go up. Keep a small, permanent, paid human sample from day one.

05

Why the hybrid makes more money than either half

Say the client currently pays an outsourcing firm $14 to get one job done. You offer $10 — they save 29% and sign. Now watch what happens to your side of that $10 over four years, with the price never changing.

Why this beats the pure-agent version

  • You can start on Monday. Day one can be 80% people. You don't have to wait for the agents to be good — you just have to be cheaper than the incumbent vendor, which people alone can already manage offshore.
  • The client isn't betting on AI. They're buying a finished job with an SLA. If the agent fails, that's your problem, not theirs. This removes the single biggest objection in the room.
  • Your margin grows without a price rise. A software company has to sell more seats or charge more. You just improve the mix. There is no awkward renewal conversation.
  • Every model release helps you. Better models push the agent share up, which drops your cost while the price stays put. You benefit from other people's R&D.
  • It survives being wrong about AI. If agents plateau at 60%, you're still a profitable outsourcing business with unusually good tooling. The pure-agent version has no floor.
This is the version I'd actually build. The pure-agent marketplace is where it ends up in year six — not where it starts.
06

What you sell on day one

One sentence, and it should not contain the word AI:

The pitch

"You pay $14 a packet today and wait four days. We'll do it for $10 and get it back to you same day. Same accuracy, guaranteed in the contract. Start with 10% of your volume and check our work."

That's it. No demo, no platform, no pilot. A price, a turnaround, and a guarantee — the three things an operations buyer has been comparing vendors on for thirty years. Whether a person or an agent does the work is your business, not theirs, and it is the thing you get to change quietly every month.

The software you build to run this crew is the thing you sell at rung 2, the marketplace you build on rung 3, and the rail you become at rung 4. But none of that is what a customer buys in year one, and building it before you have customers is how this goes wrong.
