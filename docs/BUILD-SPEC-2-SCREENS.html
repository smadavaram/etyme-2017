Where the diamond fits, where the loop fits, and how Claude designs the front end from this page.

1

All four shapes are in here. They stack.

The video framed the four shapes as a menu — pick one. In a real product they nest inside each other. Your staffing app uses all four, and knowing which goes where is most of the engineering.

Chain

one after another

Each step needs the last step's output. No judgement, nothing to parallelise.

Building the submission package. Assembling a compliance pack.

Diamond

many at once, then join

Lots of independent things to judge, and waiting for them one at a time is unusable.

Scoring the bench. Screening 100 submissions. Scoring one person on five axes.

Branch

pick a lane

Only where the lane is written down as a fact. Never where something has to guess.

W2 vs corp-to-corp vs 1099. Contract vs contract-to-hire.

Loop

check, fix, check again

Where being wrong is expensive and correctness can be tested.

Submission check. Screening check. Requirement quality check.

2

The diamond, and the trap inside it

The diamond is the right shape for scoring, and it's the shape most likely to bankrupt you if you use it lazily.

Where the diamond genuinely belongs

WhereFan out acrossWhy not one at a time
Scoring the bench15 shortlisted consultantsSerial scoring of a 200-person bench takes seven minutes. Nobody waits seven minutes
Screening submissions100 submissions on one roleSame reason, other side. The client wants a shortlist today
Scoring one person5 separate questionsSkills, rate, availability, work permit, and "is this CV inflated?" are different questions with different failure modes
Sending a requirementevery vendor on the tierObviously parallel. No thought required
Double-checking the top 33 independent scorersOnly for candidates that reach the shortlist. Agreement is worth paying for on those

The one that's worth understanding properly

Row three is the interesting one. Instead of one big prompt asking "how good is this person for this job", run five small ones at the same time:

LensAsksDone by
SkillsIs each required skill actually evidenced in the CV?model
RateIs their floor inside the client's range?arithmetic
AvailabilityAre they free in the start window?date maths
Right to workValid permit, unexpired, correct type?rules
HonestyDoes this CV look inflated or copied?model, sceptical

Three of those five need no model at all. Each one is small, cheap, separately testable and separately fixable. When your matching is wrong you'll know which lens was wrong — which you never learn from one big prompt.

The trap: fanning out over everything.

A 200-person bench × 40 requirements a day is 8,000 model calls daily, per vendor. That's a bill that eats the whole business.

Filter first with plain rules — skill overlap, city, rate band, availability window. Two hundred becomes about fifteen. Then fan out. Same answer, a tenth of the cost, and it comes back in twenty seconds instead of seven minutes.

The skill isn't picking the shape. It's knowing when not to fan out at all.

Where branch does not belong.

Never branch on something a model guessed. If the system has to work out which lane a thing belongs in, that's a score with a confidence, not a branch.

Branch only on facts already written down: contract type, tier, country. Those you can read off the record, and they don't change under you.

3

Claude as the front-end architect

Yes — and there's a neat symmetry in it. The way Claude builds your front end is the same loop graph the product itself runs on.

Why the second pass matters more than the first

Any AI will build you a decent-looking screen from one sentence. What it reliably forgets is the empty state, the loading state, the error state, and what happens on a phone.

Those are exactly the states your users hit first. A brand new vendor opens the bench board and it is empty — that screen is their entire first impression of your product, and it's the one nobody specs.

So specify the empty state before the full one.

Every screen below names its empty state on purpose. In an early product the empty screen is the one most people will see, and it's where you tell them what to do next.

5

Hand this to the architect

Give Claude the design rules once, then the screens one at a time. Never all eleven at once — you'll get eleven mediocre screens instead of one good one.

ROLE
You are the front-end architect for a contract staffing app.
Build ONE screen per turn. Do not start the next until I say so.

DESIGN RULES — apply to every screen, never restate them to me
Type      Archivo for all headings, labels and numbers.
          Source Serif for body text. IBM Plex Mono for data,
          rates, dates and IDs.
Colour    Neutral greys with a slight teal cast. Deep teal for the
          client side, violet for the vendor side, burnt orange for
          anything the user should act on. Green means passed, red
          means failed. Nothing else is coloured.
Numbers   Always tabular-nums when digits sit in a column.
Density   This is an operations tool, not a marketing page. Rows
          over cards. Show more, scroll less.
Themes    Define every colour as a CSS variable on :root, redefine
          them for dark mode, and never write a colour inline.
State     Encode status as a shape as well as a word — a pill,
          a stripe, a badge — so it reads without being read.

FOR EVERY SCREEN, BUILD ALL FOUR STATES
1. Empty — a first-time user with no data. Say what to do next.
2. Loading — skeleton rows, never a spinner on its own.
3. Full — realistic staffing data, never lorem ipsum.
4. Error — what broke and what to do, no apology.

THEN CHECK YOUR OWN WORK BEFORE SHOWING ME
Re-read the spec and confirm, item by item:
- every field in the spec is on the screen
- all four states exist
- it works at 375px wide with no sideways scroll
- text passes 4.5:1 contrast in light AND dark mode
- every control reachable by keyboard, with a visible focus ring
Fix anything failing, then check again. Three passes maximum,
then tell me what you could not fix and why.
